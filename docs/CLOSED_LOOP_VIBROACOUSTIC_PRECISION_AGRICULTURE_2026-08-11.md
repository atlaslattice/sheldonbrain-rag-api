## Abstract
This paper specifies a closed-loop vibroacoustic precision-agriculture architecture derived from an earlier program of vibroacoustic sanitation, drone-assisted building maintenance, mold/hive suppression, and integrated high-rise agriculture. The new specialization treats agricultural pest and pathogen management as a sensing, classification, targeted-actuation, verification, and learning problem rather than as indiscriminate chemical treatment.

The central hypothesis is that selected pests, fungal/mold processes, and other unwanted biological activity may be detected through multimodal sensing and influenced by experimentally validated acoustic or substrate-borne vibration regimes while minimizing effects on crops, beneficial organisms, humans, and surrounding ecosystems.

The system is explicitly experimental. No universal pest-killing frequency is assumed. No claim is made that resonance can safely destroy arbitrary insects or pathogens. Frequency, amplitude, waveform, duty cycle, spatial targeting, and ecological side effects are empirical variables.

The Founder’s prior work supplies the architectural substrate: vibroacoustic sanitation, autonomous drones, sensing, regenerative buildings, and integrated agriculture. ## 2. Design principle
`DETECT → CLASSIFY → LOCALIZE → SELECT TREATMENT → ACTUATE → VERIFY → LEARN`

The objective is not maximum acoustic power. It is **minimum-impact control**: achieve a predefined biological or agricultural outcome using the lowest effective acoustic energy, smallest treated volume, shortest exposure, and least ecological collateral effect.

## 3. System boundary
The module covers agricultural pest detection and deterrence/control; mold/fungal detection and experimentally supported suppression; substrate-borne vibration monitoring; crop/plant mechanical and physiological response monitoring; localized airborne acoustic actuation; localized structural/substrate vibration actuation; drone/robot positioning; integration with conventional biological, mechanical, environmental and chemical controls; and longitudinal telemetry.

It does not assume that insects as a class should be removed. Broader ecological hypotheses concerning insect-free or predator-free systems are evaluated separately under controlled simulation and staged experimentation.

## 4. Sensor fusion
Candidate layers include machine vision; macro/microscopic imaging; microphones; piezoelectric/contact vibration sensors; accelerometers and strain sensors; thermal and multispectral imaging; humidity, temperature, soil moisture, leaf wetness, wind and CO2 telemetry; validated VOC sensors; eDNA/biological sampling; and conventional traps as ground truth.

**Sentinel organisms are preferred to indiscriminate instrumentation of every leaf.** A representative plant can become a multimodal ecological observatory without coating an entire ecosystem in electronics.

## 5. Actuation
### 5.1 Airborne acoustics
Directional loudspeakers, ultrasonic devices where independently supported for a target, and low-frequency arrays may be tested. Professional beamforming/phased-array methods are candidate tools when spatial selectivity matters.

### 5.2 Substrate-borne vibration
Piezoelectric actuators, shakers, structural exciters or plant-mounted experimental actuators may inject controlled vibration into stems, trellises, beds, hydroponic structures, greenhouse frames or other substrates. Many insects communicate and sense through plant-borne vibration, making this an especially interesting experimental channel.

### 5.3 Mobile actuation
Drones and ground robots can move sensors or compact actuators close to a localized target, reducing required acoustic power and avoiding whole-field exposure.

### 5.4 Environmental control
Humidity, airflow, irrigation, temperature, pruning, sanitation, filtration, UV or other validated treatments may be coordinated with vibroacoustics. Acoustics are a candidate force multiplier, not a mandatory replacement for proven controls.

## 6. Control parameters
Record target organism/process and confidence; crop and developmental stage; frequency/bandwidth; waveform; harmonic spectrum; SPL and/or substrate acceleration/velocity at target; phase/array geometry; duration/duty cycle; distance/orientation; environmental conditions; simultaneous non-acoustic treatment; target response; crop response; non-target response; recurrence time; and energy use.

No special status is assigned to 432 Hz, 11 Hz, or any other frequency without comparative experimental evidence.

## 7. Experimental ladder
**Phase A — Bench characterization:** contained dose-response sweeps with sham and conventional controls.

**Phase B — Plant-level trials:** randomized matched plants; measure burden, growth, flowering/fruiting, damage, water use and recovery.

**Phase C — Mesocosm/greenhouse:** add ecological complexity, realistic airflow/humidity, beneficial organisms and repeated exposure; test adaptation and non-target effects.

**Phase D — Building-integrated agriculture:** greenhouse floors, atria, vertical farms and regenerative skyscraper gardens integrated with drones, HVAC, irrigation and sanitation telemetry.

**Phase E — Bounded field trials:** only treatments demonstrating selectivity and safety graduate outdoors; replicated treated/control plots with predefined stop criteria.

## 8. Falsifiable hypotheses
- **H1:** multimodal acoustic/vibration sensing improves early detection of specified pest activity relative to visual inspection alone.
- **H2:** for at least one defined pest/crop pair, targeted vibroacoustic treatment reduces pest activity or reproductive success relative to sham treatment without reducing crop performance.
- **H3:** closed-loop localized actuation uses less energy and causes fewer non-target exposures than continuous broad-area acoustic treatment for an equivalent outcome.
- **H4:** for at least one defined mold/fungal process, vibroacoustic augmentation improves a conventional environmental/sanitation intervention relative to that intervention alone.
- **H5:** longitudinal telemetry detects adaptation or treatment resistance early enough to retire ineffective waveforms rather than escalating blindly.

**Null results are successful results for the knowledge system.** If acoustics provide no useful advantage for a target, the controller should learn not to use them.

## 9. Safety and ecological selectivity
Enforce exposure limits for humans/animals; avoid demonstrated harmful regimes; monitor beneficial and non-target species; prevent uncontrolled high-SPL experimentation; and include shutdown criteria for crop damage, wildlife disturbance, structural resonance, human discomfort or unexpected ecological effects.

Agricultural control differs fundamentally from building sanitation because the target exists inside a living production ecosystem. **Selectivity is a primary performance metric.**

## 10. Data and Receipts
Every experiment writes a versioned record containing inputs, configuration, raw telemetry references, transformations, outcomes, uncertainty, failures and operator/model decisions. Negative findings remain searchable. Historical baselines are preserved to avoid shifted-baseline errors.

Epistemic labels: `ESTABLISHED`, `SUPPORTED`, `CANDIDATE`, `HYPOTHESIS`, `REJECTED`.

## 11. Relationship to fire resilience
The module shares thermal/optical sensing, phased acoustics, environmental control, drones, mesh telemetry and closed-loop optimization with Distributed Vibroacoustic Fire Resilience. Fire suppression optimizes rapid extinction/prevention of ignition growth; precision agriculture optimizes selective long-term biological control while preserving crop and ecosystem function.

## 13. Implementation spec — `bioacoustic_pest_control`
### Inputs
Node/building location; crop map; target taxonomy; sensor/actuator inventories; historical infestation/pathogen data; weather/HVAC state; ecological exclusions; occupancy; energy budget; treatment library; confidence thresholds.

### Runtime
1. Detect anomaly.
2. Fuse sensor evidence.
3. Classify target and uncertainty.
4. Confirm treatment authorization for target/environment.
5. Localize target volume/substrate.
6. Select minimum-impact candidate treatment.
7. Establish pre-treatment baseline.
8. Actuate within validated envelope.
9. Measure immediate response.
10. Continue, modify or abort against predefined thresholds.
11. Monitor delayed effects and recurrence.
12. Write complete record to ledger.
13. Update treatment confidence only after sufficient replicated evidence.

### Outputs
Target burden/activity change; crop-health change; non-target effects; treatment energy/duration; spatial exposure; recurrence time; confidence interval; recommendation status.

## 15. Conclusion
Closed-loop vibroacoustic precision agriculture is not proposed as a universal sonic pesticide. It is an experimental control architecture combining multimodal biological detection, localized acoustic/substrate actuation, ecological selectivity, mobile robotics and rigorous outcome telemetry inside a learning loop derived from an existing regenerative-building sanitation architecture.

If a waveform works, preserve the receipt. If it fails, preserve that receipt too. **The goal is not to prove that vibroacoustics can control everything. The goal is to build an agricultural system capable of finding out, safely and reproducibly.**
